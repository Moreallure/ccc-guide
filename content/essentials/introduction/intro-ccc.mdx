# What is the Canadian Computing Competition (CCC)?

The Canadian Computing Competition (CCC) is a programming contest for high school students run by the University of Waterloo’s Centre for Education in Mathematics and Computing (CEMC). You get three hours to work through five problems on your own. Each problem is worth 15 marks, for a total of 75.

## 2027 CCC dates and registration

- **February 17, 2027**: North and South America
- **February 18, 2027**: Other regions

Ask a teacher at your school if you’d like to participate. Your school orders the contest by **February 11, 2027**, and a teacher approves your grader account and supervises you on contest day. The CEMC keeps the dates, rules, and fees on the [official CCC page](https://cemc.uwaterloo.ca/contests/ccc).

## CCC Junior vs Senior

Junior is a good place to start if most of your programs use loops, conditions, and lists. The first two problems usually draw on those basics. By J4 or J5, you might have to work with a grid or find a faster way to handle a large input.

Senior is worth trying if you’re comfortable with the later Junior problems and have worked with algorithms for tasks like sorting and searching. Your grade doesn’t decide which level you write. If you’re unsure, try J3 and S1 from a past contest and see how far you get on each.

We use **J1–J5** for Junior and **S1–S5** for Senior. When you see **2024 J4**, that means the fourth Junior problem from 2024.

## Judging and languages

During the contest, you submit your code to the [CCC Online Grader](https://cccgrader.com/). It runs your program on hidden inputs and checks the output. The grader expects exactly what the problem asks for, so prompts like `Enter a number:` are not needed.

Some problems have **subtasks**. A solution that’s too slow for the full problem can still earn marks if it handles the smaller cases in a subtask. In 2026, the average Senior score was 15.56 out of 75. One full solution and a few partial marks would have put you above that year’s average.

The grader accepts C, C++, Python 2, Python 3, and Java. Start with the language you know best. [Choosing a Language](/essentials/introduction/choosing-a-language) explains when the time limits might affect that choice.

## Beyond the contest

The top quarter of contestants earn a Certificate of Distinction. Some of the top official Senior contestants appear on the honour roll or are invited to the Canadian Computing Olympiad (CCO) or the Canadian Informatics Workshop (CIW). Any full-time student can write the CCC. Check the [CEMC’s eligibility rules](https://cemc.uwaterloo.ca/contests/ccc) if you’re aiming for the honour roll or an invitation.

## How to prepare for the CCC

- **Worked examples.** [The First 30 Marks](/junior/warm-ups/first-30-marks) explains the first two questions from the 2026 Junior contest and shows how to solve each one. If you’re preparing for Senior or want to study a particular technique, [browse topics](/topics) to see where it comes up in past contests and find related lessons.
- **Past problems.** Search the [CCC Guide problem archive](/problems) by year or problem name, or filter by level and topic. For a full contest paper or an official solution, see the [CEMC past contests archive](https://cemc.uwaterloo.ca/resources/past-contests).
- **Practice submissions.** Open a question in the archive and follow its [DMOJ](https://dmoj.ca/) link to submit your code. DMOJ runs your solution against its test cases and reports the result.

If you’re wondering which lessons to start with or where to learn the programming basics, see [Using This Guide](/essentials/introduction/using-this-guide). The [official CCC page](https://cemc.uwaterloo.ca/contests/ccc) also lists recommended books and workshops for schools.
